# Ensembling BPE Tokenizers: Feasibility, Benefits, and Drawbacks

## Introduction

Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) is a ubiquitous subword tokenization method used in language models[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04653v1#:~:text=Philip%20Gage%20introduced%20the%20byte,changes%20to%20the%20tokenization%20approach). It builds a vocabulary of subword tokens by greedily merging the most frequent symbol pairs in a corpus until a target vocabulary size is reached[stackoverflow.com](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/63263882/bpe-multiple-ways-to-encode-a-word#:~:text=The%20BPE%20algorithm%20learns%20the,encoding%20process%20for%20new%20text)[stackoverflow.com](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/63263882/bpe-multiple-ways-to-encode-a-word#:~:text=Encoding%20the%20new%20text%20,will%20be%20encoded%20as%20follows). This greedy, frequency-driven process optimizes compression on the training data, but it also has known limitations: it can overfit to idiosyncratic corpus patterns, include rare or under-utilized tokens, and underrepresent minority domains or languages[aiml.com](https://aiml.com/demystifying-byte-pair-encoding-bpe/#:~:text=6)[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04599#:~:text=,in%20several%20cases%20improves%20it). The question at hand is whether training multiple BPE tokenizers on different chunks of data and **ensembling** their merge rules (e.g. by voting on merges or merging their vocabularies) can produce a better tokenizer – one that generalizes more broadly or balances diverse data characteristics.

This report surveys relevant literature and evidence for and against the idea of an ensemble-trained BPE tokenizer. We consider theoretical expectations and any empirical findings related to this approach, weighing the pros and cons. We also discuss different use cases – from pure compression and data storage to training small efficient models or large multilingual models, and across natural language, code, or binary data – since the utility of an ensemble tokenizer may vary by scenario. In the end, we provide a comparative “scorecard” of the evidence and offer a reasoned conclusion on whether the ensemble concept is promising.

## Background: BPE and Its Limitations

**BPE Recap:** In standard BPE, we start with a base vocabulary (often all characters or bytes), then iteratively replace the most frequent adjacent pair of symbols with a new merged token[stackoverflow.com](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/63263882/bpe-multiple-ways-to-encode-a-word#:~:text=The%20BPE%20algorithm%20learns%20the,encoding%20process%20for%20new%20text). The result is a merge list (rules applied in a fixed order) and a final vocabulary of a specified size. This greedy algorithm is effective and simple, but it *locally* optimizes frequency at each step rather than any global objective[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.414#:~:text=pretraining,over%20the%20more%20prevalent%20BPE). Research has shown that this can lead to suboptimal subword units compared to more global methods like Unigram LM segmentation[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.414#:~:text=pretraining,over%20the%20more%20prevalent%20BPE)[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.414#:~:text=with%20morphology%20and%20avoids%20problems,we%20find%20that%20the). For example, BPE’s merges often do not align well with linguistic morphemes due to its greedy construction, whereas the Unigram model finds subwords that better match true morphological units[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.414#:~:text=pretraining,over%20the%20more%20prevalent%20BPE)[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.414#:~:text=with%20morphology%20and%20avoids%20problems,we%20find%20that%20the).

**Known Issues:** Several studies and reports highlight relevant BPE issues that motivate exploring alternative training strategies:

* *Overfitting to Training Corpus:* BPE is essentially a compression scheme; it maximally compresses the training text. This means it will create tokens for very frequent patterns specific to that corpus. As a result, it compresses the training data more than unseen data. Indeed, it’s observed that tokenization compresses its training text better than it does test text from a slightly different distribution[aiml.com](https://aiml.com/demystifying-byte-pair-encoding-bpe/#:~:text=print%28f%E2%80%9DTrain%20compression%3A%20%7Btrain_ratio%3A.2f%7D,%E2%80%9D)[aiml.com](https://aiml.com/demystifying-byte-pair-encoding-bpe/#:~:text=,resulting%20in%20slightly%20worse%20compression). In domain shift scenarios (e.g. using an English-trained tokenizer on legal text or on another language), BPE yields longer token sequences and worse compression, since it failed to learn subwords suited to the new data[aiml.com](https://aiml.com/demystifying-byte-pair-encoding-bpe/#:~:text=2.%20Domain%20%26%20Distribution%20,resulting%20in%20longer%20token%20sequences)[aiml.com](https://aiml.com/demystifying-byte-pair-encoding-bpe/#:~:text=As%20expected%2C%20English%20and%20languages,to%20avoid%20inefficiencies%20and%20biases). In other words, a tokenizer heavily fit to one domain may generalize poorly to others.
* *Greedy Merge Suboptimality:* BPE can pick merges that are too domain-specific or overly specific, at the expense of more general merges. As one analysis puts it, “greedy merging can be suboptimal if the corpus is small or domain-specific merges overshadow more general merges you might want”[aiml.com](https://aiml.com/demystifying-byte-pair-encoding-bpe/#:~:text=6). For example, if a particular bigram is extremely frequent in the training set (perhaps a quirk of a specific source or genre), BPE will eagerly make it a token. This might save a few characters in that training set, but if that pattern is rare outside the training data, the resulting token might be unused or unnecessary elsewhere. A more *general* merge (like a common suffix or root) could have been more useful across a broader text distribution, but the greedy algorithm might miss it because of that dominant domain-specific pattern.
* *Rare and Under-trained Tokens:* Because BPE will include some merges even for moderately frequent pairs, the final vocabulary often contains tokens that appear only a handful of times in the training corpus. These low-frequency tokens present problems for model training – they are “under-trained” in the sense that the language model sees them too rarely to learn a good embedding[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04599#:~:text=,in%20several%20cases%20improves%20it)[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04599#:~:text=reliable%20method,Our). Chizhov et al. (2024) specifically identify *under-trained tokens* as an issue caused by classical BPE, noting it can lead to suboptimal compression and even affect downstream performance[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04599#:~:text=,in%20several%20cases%20improves%20it). An empirical study by *BPE Gets Picky* demonstrated that refining the BPE vocabulary to remove such tokens can improve efficiency without hurting compression or model accuracy[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04599#:~:text=,in%20several%20cases%20improves%20it)[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04599#:~:text=eliminates%20under,in%20several%20cases%20improves%20it). In their modified “Picky BPE”, they eliminate merges that lead to rarely used tokens, thereby **“eliminat(ing) under-trained tokens, and (doing) so without compromising text compression or downstream performance”[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04599#:~:text=,in%20several%20cases%20improves%20it)**. This suggests that a more judicious selection of merges (beyond pure frequency count) can yield a better vocabulary.
* *Multilingual and Multi-domain Challenges:* In joint tokenization of multiple languages or domains, a single BPE model might not allocate vocabulary in an optimal way for all segments. If one language or domain dominates the training data, it will also dominate the merge decisions. Consequently, languages with fewer data get fewer dedicated subword tokens and suffer over-fragmentation (each word broken into many pieces)[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=retrieval%2C%20NLI,models%20and%20guidelines%20for%20future). Conversely, sharing too many tokens across languages can be harmful in some cases: a study on multilingual models found that *excessive overlap* of vocabulary between languages was **detrimental for word-level tasks like part-of-speech tagging and dependency parsing**, which rely on language-specific morphemes[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=In%20this%20paper%2C%20we%20propose,We%20also%20observe%20that%20the)[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=of%20vocabulary%20across%20languages%20can,lingual). On the other hand, that same vocabulary sharing helped cross-lingual tasks like named entity recognition (NER) and sentence retrieval, which benefit from common tokens for similar names or shared terms[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=vocabulary%20overlap%20observed%20in%20sub,in%20the%20multilin%02gual%20vocabulary%20significantly). This indicates a trade-off: a tokenizer must balance shared subwords versus language-specific subwords. Techniques like **NoOverlap vs. Overlap** vocabularies have been studied: e.g., training separate monolingual tokenizers and then *merging identical tokens* (so-called “TokMix” approach) versus training one joint tokenizer[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=,Tokenizer%20and%20Model%20Training%20Setting). The NoOverlap extreme (completely separate vocabularies per language) was found to give the best performance on POS tagging and syntactic tasks, presumably because it preserves distinct morphemes for each language[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=Notably%2C%20the%20model%20instance%20with,for%20tokens%20that%20is%20especially). In fact, a fully disjoint vocabulary model achieved the highest POS/dependency scores despite each language getting a smaller sub-vocab, highlighting the benefit of language-specific tokens in those tasks[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=Notably%2C%20the%20model%20instance%20with,for%20tokens%20that%20is%20especially). Researchers attributed this to avoiding conflating tokens that look the same but mean different things across languages (for example, the token "a" is an English article vs. a Spanish preposition)[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=NOOVERLAP%20approach%20obtains%20competitive%20results,We%20think). This example shows how a shared tokenizer might incorrectly treat these as one token, whereas separate tokenizers keep them distinct, aiding clarity[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=NOOVERLAP%20approach%20obtains%20competitive%20results,We%20think).

**Motivation for an Ensemble BPE:** The idea of ensemble-training a tokenizer arises naturally from these observations. If one could train multiple BPE models on different portions or aspects of the data and then combine their vocabularies or merge rules, perhaps we can capture a broader set of subword patterns while filtering out the idiosyncratic or over-specialized ones. In effect, this would be akin to *bagging* in machine learning: each model (tokenizer) sees a subset of data, and by averaging or voting their results, we might get a more robust overall tokenizer less prone to overfitting any single subset. We could, for example, partition the corpus into *N* chunks (which could be disjoint, overlapping, or stratified by domain/language) and train *M* separate BPE tokenizers (where maybe $M=N$, one per chunk). Each is trained to a certain merge count or vocab size *V*. Then we need a way to **ensemble** them – for instance, by letting them vote on the merge order (choosing merges that appear in most models first), or by taking the union/intersection of the token sets they produce and possibly retraining or reordering merges accordingly. The final vocabulary could be the same size as each individual (*F = V*), or potentially smaller (*F < V* if we take only consensus merges), or larger if we simply take a union (though the prompt emphasizes $F \le V$).

Crucially, ensembling could be done with equal weighting for each chunk (treat each sub-tokenizer’s choices equally), or with weighted influence (e.g. larger weight to merges coming from the larger or more informative chunks). One might even weight by an entropy measure – giving more say to tokenizers trained on diverse/high-entropy text to avoid overemphasizing a repetitive corpus portion. This ensemble concept intersects with ideas from federated learning (learning a tokenizer from distributed data) and data augmentation (exposing a model to multiple tokenizations). While this exact idea is not standard, we can draw on several related research threads to predict its pros and cons:

* **Vocabulary Merging in Multilingual Contexts:** There are real examples of combining tokenizers. For instance, to expand an English tokenizer for Japanese text, one might train a separate Japanese BPE and then merge the two vocabularies[github.com](https://github.com/google/sentencepiece/issues/830#:~:text=1,between%20English%20and%20Japanese%20vocablaries)[github.com](https://github.com/google/sentencepiece/issues/830#:~:text=Metadata). A blog tutorial by Kimamani (2023) demonstrates merging a Greek tokenizer into LLaMA-3’s English tokenizer: essentially taking the union of merges and then re-training the BPE model with the combined merge list[gucci-j.github.io](https://gucci-j.github.io/post/en/vocab-expansion/#:~:text=2,rules)[gucci-j.github.io](https://gucci-j.github.io/post/en/vocab-expansion/#:~:text=if%20merge%20in%20merges%3A%20old_merges,append%28merge). The result is an expanded vocabulary that covers Greek symbols and words, reducing over-fragmentation in Greek text[gucci-j.github.io](https://gucci-j.github.io/post/en/vocab-expansion/#:~:text=Since%20the%20advent%20of%20LLaMA2%2C,resulting%20in%20better%20inference%20efficiency)[gucci-j.github.io](https://gucci-j.github.io/post/en/vocab-expansion/#:~:text=languages,resulting%20in%20better%20inference%20efficiency). This shows it is *feasible* to merge BPE vocabularies post-hoc. However, the procedure is non-trivial – one must carefully integrate the merge lists to preserve existing encodings (e.g. ensuring English tokenization remained unchanged in that example[github.com](https://github.com/google/sentencepiece/issues/830#:~:text=1,between%20English%20and%20Japanese%20vocablaries)). The merge order had to be adjusted: new Greek merges were inserted in a way that didn’t interfere with original merges[gucci-j.github.io](https://gucci-j.github.io/post/en/vocab-expansion/#:~:text=,token%20%3D%20token_1%20%2B%20token_2)[gucci-j.github.io](https://gucci-j.github.io/post/en/vocab-expansion/#:~:text=if%20merge%20in%20merges%3A%20old_merges,append%28merge). This underscores that any ensemble method must handle **merge order conflicts**: different BPEs may have made incompatible choices (one might merge `a+b` while another merges `b+c`; if both rules are included, which applies first?).
* **Batching and Distributed BPE Training:** Recent work by Morgan (2024) on *BatchBPE* shows that BPE merges can be parallelized without loss in quality by merging multiple pairs in each iteration (when those pairs occur in disjoint contexts)[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04653v1#:~:text=1,2%20Filtering%20rare%20text%20chunks)[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04653v1#:~:text=The%20Byte%20Pair%20Encoding%20algorithm,the%20batch%20merging%20process%20and). While this is aimed at speeding up training rather than ensembling, it demonstrates that BPE’s outcome can be reached through different pathways (safe out-of-order merges) as long as the frequent pairs are accounted for. In a sense, splitting the data and merging results is an extreme case of out-of-order merges – if done carefully, it could approximate the same result as a single unified training. However, naive splits might miss cross-chunk pair frequency interactions. Morgan emphasizes that tokenization remains a field with “a lot of room for improvement” and notes that OpenAI themselves have iterated on their tokenizers (doubling vocab size and adding more languages in each major release)[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04653v1#:~:text=utilitarian%20or%20uninteresting%2C%20but%20the,AI%20research%20in%20active%20development)[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04653v1#:~:text=with%20a%20lot%20of%20room,aspect%20of%20AI%20research%20in). This indicates a willingness to adjust vocabulary construction to handle more diverse data – essentially addressing the same issues an ensemble aims to solve (e.g., ensuring more languages or domains are covered by tokens).
* **Randomized Merge Orders (Robustness):** Another relevant insight comes from Sälevä and Lignos (2023), who experimented with **randomizing** the BPE merge selection instead of always picking the single most frequent pair[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.insights-1.7.pdf#:~:text=We%20introduce%20two%20simple%20randomized,one%20variant%20performs%20nearly%20indistinguishably)[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.insights-1.7.pdf#:~:text=the%20step%20of%20picking%20the,each%20iteration%20with%20random%20sampling). They introduced stochastic variations of BPE (softmax sampling and uniform random selection of merges) to see if different merge choices would affect downstream model performance in machine translation. Their *surprising* finding was that it doesn’t matter much – *“subword vocabularies created with randomized BPE yield translation models that perform comparably to those using the standard greedy BPE”*, and even a completely uniform-random merge choice only significantly hurt performance in 2 out of 4 languages tested[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.insights-1.7.pdf#:~:text=We%20introduced%20two%20randomized%20variants,This). In most cases, random BPE was as good as standard BPE for translation quality[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.insights-1.7.pdf#:~:text=We%20introduced%20two%20randomized%20variants,This). This negative result suggests that there is a whole space of possible tokenizers that are about equally effective for the model, as long as they produce a reasonable coverage of subwords. It implies that BPE’s greedy choice isn’t uniquely optimal; many different merge orders (and hence vocabularies) yield similar outcomes[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.insights-1.7.pdf#:~:text=yield%20translation%20models%20that%20perform,it%20sug%02gests%20that%20variations%20on). For our discussion, this has two implications: (1) it tempers expectations that an ensemble-derived tokenizer would dramatically improve model performance – since even fairly perturbed tokenizers were fine, the bar for improvement is high; but (2) it encourages the idea of ensemble because it indicates that *some* flexibility in merge selection doesn’t inherently break the tokenizer’s utility. If random differences don’t hurt much, then combining multiple merge lists (which is a structured kind of difference) might also produce a viable tokenizer. In short, the exact merge order may not be critical, as long as the resulting vocabulary has the frequent pieces; an ensemble approach would aim to ensure it has the *right* pieces (frequent and general ones).
* **Subword Regularization:** Separately, it’s worth noting methods like SentencePiece’s subword regularization (Kudo, 2018) and BPE-dropout (Provilkov et al., 2020), which use *multiple tokenization variants during model training* to improve generalization. Those techniques are not about creating a single final tokenizer via ensemble, but they acknowledge a similar principle: there isn’t one “correct” segmentation, and exposing the model to diverse segmentations can be beneficial. In practice, one could simulate an ensemble of tokenizers by sampling different BPE segmentations at training time. This has been shown to improve translation and language modeling robustness[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1007.pdf#:~:text=,provements%20over%20the). It lends indirect support to the idea that a tokenizer that somehow incorporates broader knowledge (or at least avoids committing to one possibly suboptimal segmentation) could be advantageous.

With this context, we now examine the pros and cons of an ensemble-based BPE training strategy.

## Potential Advantages of an Ensemble BPE Tokenizer

**1. Better Generalization and Robustness:** The primary argument for ensembling is to avoid overfitting the tokenizer to peculiarities of the training set. By training on multiple subsets and combining, we may preferentially select merges that are common across different slices of data – in effect, merges that *generalize*. Hypothetically, a merge that only one subset found useful (perhaps because that subset had a burst of a specific phrase) would be outvoted by merges that appear in many subsets. This could filter out highly corpus-specific tokens, leading to a more *robust vocabulary* that compresses a variety of texts reasonably well instead of compressing one text extremely well and others poorly. For example, if we ensemble tokenizers trained on different time periods or sources, the final vocabulary might exclude slang or boilerplate unique to one source, focusing on more enduring subwords. This addresses the “train vs. test compression gap” – potentially narrowing the difference so that the tokenizer isn’t overly specialized to the training distribution. While we did not find a direct study doing this exact experiment, it aligns with the observations that BPE can overfit small corpora[aiml.com](https://aiml.com/demystifying-byte-pair-encoding-bpe/#:~:text=6) and that eliminating rare tokens improves downstream usage[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04599#:~:text=,in%20several%20cases%20improves%20it). An ensemble inherently leans toward eliminating tokens that lack support in multiple data portions (a rough analog to requiring a minimum frequency across data splits). This could also mean fewer under-trained tokens: if a token doesn’t consistently appear, it might not survive the voting. *Picky BPE* results suggest removing low-support tokens has no loss in compression and can even **improve model efficiency and some performance**[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04599#:~:text=,in%20several%20cases%20improves%20it)[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04599#:~:text=eliminates%20under,in%20several%20cases%20improves%20it). Thus, an ensemble tokenizer might naturally implement a kind of frequency thresholding, increasing vocabulary *efficiency*. In practical terms, that could benefit small models which can’t afford many wasted embedding vectors – they’d get a leaner, more meaningful subword set.

**2. Coverage of Diverse Domains/Languages:** By splitting the corpus along different domains or languages, an ensemble can ensure each sub-corpus gets representation in the final vocabulary. A single BPE run on a mixed corpus will allocate merges according to overall frequency, which can lead to dominance by the largest or easiest-to-compress domain. Ensemble training treats each portion (potentially each domain) separately first. This way, even a smaller domain can generate up to *V* merges tailored to its content. When we combine, the union of tokens (or the voted merges) would include many of those domain-specific tokens that might never have been chosen if the domain were pooled with a much larger one. This approach resonates with the **“balance vs. efficiency”** issue in multilingual models. Research shows that simply mixing all languages can hurt smaller languages – they end up with broken-up words and lower accuracy on tasks[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=retrieval%2C%20NLI,models%20and%20guidelines%20for%20future). An ensemble approach (with equal or weighted voting per language) is a way to enforce a more balanced vocabulary allocation. If each language’s tokenizer contributes its top merges, the final merged vocabulary is effectively a weighted union. We can choose to weight languages equally (to maximize coverage for each, useful for e.g. a multilingual model serving many languages equally) or by corpus size (which leans closer to the single-BPE result). Even equal weighting could be beneficial: it’s akin to the *NoOverlap* approach but allowing some overlap if languages independently chose the same merge. Limisiewicz et al. (2023) introduced metrics for vocabulary allocation and overlap, finding that more language-specific tokens (less overlap) improved word-level linguistic tasks[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=vocabulary%20overlap%20observed%20in%20sub,in%20the%20multilin%02gual%20vocabulary%20significantly)[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=Notably%2C%20the%20model%20instance%20with,for%20tokens%20that%20is%20especially). An ensemble tokenizer naturally produces a mix of shared and language-specific tokens – shared ones will appear in multiple monolingual tokenizers and thus be in the intersection (likely voted in), whereas truly language-unique tokens will appear from only one source. Depending on whether we allow those (union) or require some support from more than one subset, we can dial the overlap. Notably, if we allow full union of all tokens (with $F$ possibly > $V$), we maximize coverage but blow up vocab size; if we require consensus, we emphasize shared tokens and might drop many language-specific ones. The sweet spot could be something like including all tokens that at least appear in *k* out of *M* tokenizers. This could preserve tokens for languages that are related or domains that share terminology, while filtering out the most niche ones. We anticipate this would particularly help *multilingual LMs or multi-domain LMs* by giving adequate representation to each domain. Empirical support comes from observations like: *“coverage of language-specific tokens significantly impacts word-level tasks”*[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=%28POS%2C%20dependency%20tree%20labeling%29,models%20and%20guidelines%20for%20future) (each language needs enough dedicated tokens) and that a tokenizer with no sharing (each language fully separate) excelled in certain tasks[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=Notably%2C%20the%20model%20instance%20with,for%20tokens%20that%20is%20especially). An ensemble method can approximate a controlled sharing: it is largely separate (since each sub-tokenizer picked its own merges) but with an opportunity to merge vocab if there’s overlap or if we explicitly decide to share some tokens across subsets (like identical punctuation, numbers, or truly common words across languages). In fact, one ensemble method (*TokMix*) explicitly merged identical tokens from monolingual vocabularies into one, to avoid duplication[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=,Tokenizer%20and%20Model%20Training%20Setting). This is essentially voting with a threshold of 2: if a subword string appears in multiple tokenizers’ output, keep one copy of it. Such a method was shown to improve some cross-lingual tasks (because it encourages a unified representation for similar tokens across languages) while still giving each language its own unique tokens for what’s not shared[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=vocabulary%20overlap%20observed%20in%20sub,in%20the%20multilin%02gual%20vocabulary%20significantly).

**3. Enhanced Stability and Confidence in Merges:** Training on the full corpus can sometimes yield merges that are sensitive to small changes in the data or tie-breaking in frequency counts. By taking a consensus of multiple trainings (especially if those trainings involve some randomness or different samples of data), we might get a more *stable* set of merges. In ensemble learning, averaging usually reduces variance. Here, variance could mean the arbitrary choices BPE might make when frequencies are close or corpus content is shuffled. If an ensemble consistently picks a certain merge as important (because it appears in many splits’ top lists), we can be more confident that merge is a fundamental pattern of the language/data, not an artifact of sampling. This is akin to estimating the “true” frequent subwords with some uncertainty quantification. For example, Gutierrez-Vasques et al. (2021) found that after only ~350 merges, many languages start to exhibit very similar subword frequency distributions[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2021.eacl-main.302/#:~:text=for%2047%20diverse%20languages,different%20types%20of%20morphological%20complexity)[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2021.eacl-main.302/#:~:text=characters%20to%20words,) – essentially the very frequent character sequences (like common syllables or simple words) are universal. Beyond that point, languages diverge. If you were to train BPE on different halves of an English corpus, the initial merges (covering characters -> bigrams -> common short subwords) would likely coincide (high consensus), whereas later merges might differ. An ensemble would clearly include those first 350-ish merges, since all tokenizers agree on them, and then among later merges it might pick only those that aren’t purely one subset’s fluke. This could yield a tokenizer that captures the “core” subword structure of the language with high fidelity. It’s a bit like building an intersection of multiple BPE runs to get a set of merges that are *consistently useful*. Such stability might be useful for reproducibility and fairness: it reduces the chance that an unlucky sampling of training data yields a weird token that another sampling would not have chosen. (In practice, standard BPE on very large corpora is quite stable for high-frequency merges, but on smaller corpora this could matter.)

* *Analogy to Random BPE:* The random-BPE experiments support this notion: since many merge orders work similarly, focusing on the overlaps among them might not hurt performance. If anything, it might remove some of the variation that doesn’t matter much. The result that random variations “perform comparably” to greedy BPE[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.insights-1.7.pdf#:~:text=We%20introduced%20two%20randomized%20variants,This) suggests that as long as we get the frequent pieces in there, the model copes. Therefore, an ensemble which double-confirms those frequent pieces (they will appear in every sub-run) should be solid. And by potentially discarding pieces that were only infrequently chosen, we might lose little of value.

**4. Fairness and Mitigation of Biases:** In cases where the corpus is a mix of content (different demographics, topics, etc.), a straight BPE might over-represent the largest or most repetitive category. An ensemble could be designed to avoid that by giving each category its own tokenizer. For instance, consider an internet crawl that has a lot of one kind of text (say, forum chatter) and a smaller portion of another (literature). A single BPE might merge lots of slang and emoji from the forums and less from literature. If we train two tokenizers and ensemble, we ensure literature has a say in the vocabulary. This might mitigate bias where the tokenizer itself can introduce subtle biases – e.g., by having granular tokens for concepts common in one domain but not for others. It’s known that tokenization can encode biases (like having a single token for some names but not others, etc.). By partitioning data (even in multiple random splits) and ensembling, extremely domain-specific tokens might be filtered out, possibly leading to a fairer representation. (This is a qualitative benefit that’s hard to measure, but the idea is the ensemble could act as a regularizer.)

**5. Scalability and Practical Convenience:** On a more practical note, if one has an extremely large corpus that cannot be easily processed in one go, splitting it into chunks and training separate BPE models could be a way to parallelize the workload. The ensemble method then becomes a strategy to approximate a huge single-model BPE by merging results from each shard. This might be relevant for projects where data is distributed (federated learning of tokenizers, or simply handling data that doesn’t fit in memory). Morgan’s *BatchBPE* work demonstrates that you can safely merge results from partial computations without losing quality[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04653v1#:~:text=The%20Byte%20Pair%20Encoding%20algorithm,the%20batch%20merging%20process%20and), at least for disjoint merge operations, which gives hope that combining complete tokenizers might also be manageable. By ensembling, one could achieve a tokenizer trained on a 10^12 token corpus by, say, training 10 tokenizers on 10^11 tokens each in parallel, then merging – rather than one pass through 10^12 tokens which might be infeasible on a single machine. As long as the merge combination procedure is well-defined (which is a big “if”, see cons), this could drastically reduce wall-clock time for training a tokenizer. In resource-constrained environments, one could even train each sub-tokenizer on a random subset (like bootstrapping samples of the data) and ensemble, to approximate training on the full set with much less computation per model. There isn’t published research directly on “federated BPE training,” but this is conceptually a benefit.

In summary, **the case *for* an ensemble-trained BPE** is that it could produce a vocabulary that is more **general, balanced, and efficient**: focusing on subwords that multiple models agree on (likely truly salient subwords) and including coverage for all parts of a heterogeneous dataset. It might reduce the incidence of dubious tokens and increase the reliability of the tokenizer on new data. These advantages are especially appealing for **multilingual or multi-domain LMs**, where fairness and coverage trump raw compression of one dataset. They also align with evidence from modifications of BPE (like Picky BPE) that smarter vocabulary refinement yields equal or better downstream results with fewer useless tokens[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04599#:~:text=,in%20several%20cases%20improves%20it).

However, these potential benefits come with caveats. We must examine the challenges and downsides of this approach as indicated by theory and practice.

## Potential Drawbacks and Challenges of an Ensemble BPE Tokenizer

**1. Loss of Global Optimality in Compression:** The standard BPE algorithm, for all its greediness, is directly optimizing the overall corpus compression (each merge is the most frequent pair in the *entire* corpus at that moment). If we split the corpus and train separate models, each sub-tokenizer is optimizing compression on its chunk, not the whole. Merges that are globally optimal might not appear as top merges in any one chunk if the frequency is distributed. For example, suppose a certain bigram occurs *1000* times spread evenly across 5 chunks (200 per chunk). In each chunk, that might not be the single most frequent pair if there are other localized pairs with 300 occurrences in that chunk. So none of the individual BPEs might pick that pair early, or at all, especially if their vocabulary budgets are limited. But globally, the pair occurs 1000 times, which might have made it into a single BPE trained on all data. Thus, an ensemble could **miss important merges that only reveal their importance in aggregate**. In effect, it may underweight widespread but moderately frequent patterns in favor of highly frequent patterns that are niche to each subset. If the final vocabulary size *F* is the same as each individual *V*, then skipping such a pair could mean the ensemble model actually has worse compression or higher tokenization entropy on the whole dataset than the single model would have achieved. In the worst case, if the data splits are non-overlapping in content, each tokenizer might propose completely different merges, and an unweighted union would double-count capacity (one token from each that cover similar role). If we restrict *F = V* by selecting a subset of all merges, we have to drop many that one or another sub-tokenizer found useful. Deciding which to drop inherently sacrifices compression on some part of the data. The single BPE would have made a global trade-off that maximizes overall compression; the ensemble might make suboptimal trade-offs. In short, *“greedy global” vs. “voting local”*: there’s no guarantee the latter is optimal for the combined corpus. It might be *worse* in terms of total bits saved, which could translate to slightly longer sequences for the model to process.

* We might recall that BPE is not proven optimal globally (in fact it isn’t[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.414#:~:text=pretraining,over%20the%20more%20prevalent%20BPE)), but it’s usually reasonably good. An ensemble might deviate further from optimal. For pure compression tasks (like compressing data for storage), this is a clear downside: you’d likely get a larger compressed file if you ensemble instead of training on the full data.

**2. Implementation Complexity – Merge Conflicts and Order:** Combining multiple BPE merge lists is not straightforward. Each tokenizer yields an *ordered* list of merges (since encoding new text uses the merges in descending frequency order[stackoverflow.com](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/63263882/bpe-multiple-ways-to-encode-a-word#:~:text=frequencies%20of%20subtokens,encoding%20process%20for%20new%20text)). If we take merges from different models, how do we intermix their order in the final tokenizer? The order matters because it dictates how tokenization is performed (greedily applying merges from highest priority to lowest). Simply taking a vote on *which pairs* to include in the vocabulary is not enough; we also need a consistent ranking or application sequence for those merges. One naive way is to rank merges by the number of models that selected them (majority voting): e.g. any pair merged by all M models gets highest rank, pairs merged by M-1 of them next, etc. This provides a kind of priority. But frequency information is lost in this voting unless we incorporate it. Also, what if two merges end up in conflict? For instance, Model A merges `("t","h")->"th"` and Model B, seeing less of that, never merged `th` but instead merged `("h","e")->"he"`. If we include both "th" and "he" merges in final, which goes first will affect whether "the" becomes ("th","e") or ("t","he"). Different choices yield different final tokenizations. A unified BPE might have eventually merged "the" as a whole if frequency warranted, but our ensemble splitting might leave it ambiguous. Care must be taken to ensure the final set of merges is **consistent** (no ambiguous or overlapping merges that break determinism). The blog on vocabulary expansion[gucci-j.github.io](https://gucci-j.github.io/post/en/vocab-expansion/#:~:text=2,rules)[gucci-j.github.io](https://gucci-j.github.io/post/en/vocab-expansion/#:~:text=if%20merge%20in%20merges%3A%20old_merges,append%28merge) handled this by effectively re-running a BPE initialization: they took the combined merge list (with new merges added) and created a new tokenizer model from scratch using that list. They had to ensure no duplicate merges and that all base characters exist in the vocab, etc. In an ensemble scenario, one might need to do a final pass over the entire corpus applying the chosen merges to verify that the tokenization is well-defined and perhaps to adjust order based on actual global frequencies. At that point, however, one might ask: why not just have done a single BPE training on the whole corpus? The ensemble method could devolve into an iterative refinement where you still need to look at the full data to sort out tie-breaks or conflicts.

In practice, one could use a heuristic: **merge order by average rank.** For each candidate merge (pair of symbols), record the iteration at which each model merged it (or $\infty$ if never). Then sort merges by the average merge iteration (lower = earlier = higher frequency overall). This might approximate a global ranking. But without careful design, you could end up violating the prefix property (BPE merges should not produce a token that later gets merged as part of another token in an order that wasn’t accounted for). The ensemble merging process could introduce what we might call *merge rule collisions*.

Also, if the final vocabulary size *F* is smaller than the union of all individual merges, we face a selection problem: which merges to keep? Voting suggests merges that only one model saw might be dropped (they don’t have “consensus”). That could remove a lot of domain-specific tokens. If *F = V* exactly, we will drop roughly those merges that were least commonly selected. But is that optimal? Perhaps a rare merge from one model could be more important (in frequency) than another merge that two models happened to pick (maybe by chance). Ideally, we’d weight by frequency as well as vote count. This becomes a multi-objective selection problem – possibly solvable by assigning a score to each merge (e.g. sum of frequencies across all chunks, or sum of frequency ranks). In essence, one could replicate a single BPE by summing frequencies across chunks (which is equivalent to concatenating corpora), so if doing that, we are back to the baseline. If we instead use non-linear weighting (like giving each chunk equal influence regardless of size), then we diverge intentionally from pure frequency. But that is a design choice that might not have a clear best solution without experimentation.

In summary, the ensemble method **adds complexity** in training and especially in merging models. It’s certainly more complex than the standard pipeline (which is just: count frequencies -> do merges). Here we have to train M models, store their merges, devise a merging strategy, and perhaps retrain/verify the final tokenizer. This complexity can introduce bugs or inconsistencies, and requires careful engineering (e.g. the need to handle conflicts as described). This overhead might outweigh the benefits, especially when more straightforward methods (like simply mixing more data into one BPE or using existing adaptive tokenization techniques) are available.

**3. Risk of Underrepresenting Globally Useful Tokens:** The flip side of the earlier pro (filtering out spurious merges) is that an ensemble might filter out merges that are *legitimately useful but concentrated in one part of the data*. Consider a technical domain present as one chunk in an otherwise general corpus. That domain might have specific terminology or code patterns that *should* be learned as tokens for models to handle that domain efficiently. A single BPE on the whole corpus might indeed allocate some of the vocabulary to those patterns (proportional to their frequency in the union). But an ensemble with voting could decide those merges aren’t “universal” enough (since only the technical chunk’s tokenizer picked them) and drop them if *F < M*V (i.e., if we can’t include everything). If the final vocabulary size is the same as each individual, we inevitably will exclude some fraction of each tokenizer’s merges (unless all tokenizers chose mostly the same merges, which in heterogeneous data they wouldn’t after the common ones). This means sacrificing some domain-specific tokens. The outcome could be that those domains see increased tokenization length or unknown splits – in effect, we traded off their performance for more general performance. This might be acceptable if our goal is a one-size-fits-all tokenizer, but it *could* harm performance on that domain.

A concrete example: imagine a corpus that is 90% English and 10% programming code. A single BPE of size, say, 30k, might dedicate a few thousand tokens to code (covering common programming keywords, punctuation, maybe splits of identifiers) based on frequency. If we instead train separate 30k BPE on English and on code and then try to ensemble to 30k total, what happens? The English tokenizer will produce 30k mostly English tokens; the code tokenizer 30k mostly code tokens. The union is 60k, far above target. If we vote, the overlap (common tokens) might be only basic ASCII characters and a few common English words that appear in code comments. There might be, say, 5k overlapping or agreed tokens (like space, letters, maybe digits). Then we have 25k English-only and 25k code-only tokens competing for, say, the remaining 25k slots. How do we choose? If we equally weight, we might give ~12.5k to each – meaning the final has 5k shared + 12.5k English + 12.5k code = 30k. That would be *more* code tokens than the single BPE would have given (which maybe was 3k) – good for code domain, but it came at the cost of removing some English tokens that were not shared. Possibly those removed English tokens correspond to less frequent English words or morphemes that code’s tokenizer didn’t have. This could slightly hurt English perplexity or tasks. If instead we weight by size (English chunk is bigger, so it should contribute more tokens), we might end up with, say, ~27k English, 3k code – which is essentially back to the single BPE’s allocation. So depending on weighting, the ensemble could produce different biases. Without clear guidance, one could end up under-serving either the majority (if over-equalizing) or the minority (if just replicating frequency).

In short, ensemble introduces a *policy question*: how to trade off general vs specific tokens. There is no free lunch – a limited vocabulary cannot perfectly cover everything. A risk is that in trying to be more general, we inadvertently drop needed specific tokens and degrade performance on those specifics (for instance, out-of-vocabulary rate on domain-specific terms might increase).

**4. Minimal Empirical Gains in Some Settings:** As noted earlier, experiments with variations in tokenization often show small differences in end-task performance. BPE vs Unigram tokenization was found to yield modest but consistent advantages to Unigram LM in Bostrom & Durrett’s study[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.414#:~:text=pretraining,over%20the%20more%20prevalent%20BPE)[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.414#:~:text=with%20morphology%20and%20avoids%20problems,we%20find%20that%20the) – on the order of a couple percentage points on some fine-tuning tasks. It wasn’t a night-and-day difference, and BPE was not catastrophic by any means. The random-BPE work showed almost no difference in BLEU for MT in most cases[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.insights-1.7.pdf#:~:text=We%20introduced%20two%20randomized%20variants,This). This suggests that even if an ensemble tokenizer is conceptually more “balanced” or “robust”, it might not translate to a dramatically better language model. Large neural models are quite capable of adapting to suboptimal tokens (they can learn to spell out a rare word character by character if needed). So the improvements might be marginal or task-dependent. For example, an ensemble tokenizer might reduce the average token count for an out-of-domain dataset by a small percentage, or slightly improve how well a small model learns a rare language, but these changes could be hard to detect in overall perplexity or accuracy metrics. Meanwhile, the cost in complexity is clear.

In certain scenarios, ensemble could even hurt performance: if it removes too many specific tokens, the model might have to compose those from smaller pieces, making sequence lengths longer. For a fixed model size, longer sequences can degrade performance (since context length or positional embeddings might struggle, or simply the compute is higher). If *F < V* (final vocab smaller than each sub-vocab), we have effectively decided to use fewer tokens overall, which usually means longer sequences and potentially worse modeling of infrequent words. Unless those dropped tokens were truly useless, this could be a negative. It’s a bit like deliberately under-training the tokenizer to see more characters – which some argue could help (character-level models are more flexible) but usually at a cost of efficiency and sometimes accuracy on rare complex words.

Additionally, if we consider extremely large-scale models (like GPT-3/4 class), those often use very large vocabularies (50k to 100k tokens). At that scale, the benefit of ensembling might be negligible because the single BPE already has enough capacity to include both general and specific tokens (OpenAI, for instance, was able to simply double the vocab size to accommodate more languages[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04653v1#:~:text=with%20a%20lot%20of%20room,aspect%20of%20AI%20research%20in)). If vocabulary size is not tightly constrained, one can just increase V rather than do complex ensembling. Ensembling makes more sense when V is constrained and we want a smarter selection of V tokens. For very big models, just using all merges (i.e., union from many sub-BPEs, resulting in F > V) might be acceptable too (just train with a bigger vocab). But if one is willing to increase vocab size, simpler approaches exist, like augmenting the corpus or explicitly adding tokens for certain symbol sets.

**5. Interaction with Downstream Model Training:** Another subtle issue: if the ensemble approach yields a tokenization that is inconsistent in subtle ways from any real text distribution, it could affect modeling. For example, if we ensemble by overlapping chunks, some tokens might be composite pieces that *no single context ever saw in training*. The language model will see them, of course, but how they compose might be a bit odd. This is speculative, but consider if each sub-tokenizer had a different way of splitting a particular word, and our final tokenizer ends up mixing those merges such that it creates a segmentation no corpus chunk ever had naturally. This could happen if merges from different domains interact oddly (perhaps creating a token that mixes a prefix from one domain and a suffix from another). The model training might then have to learn that this token rarely actually appears in combination with others. However, this risk is relatively low if we stick to meaningful merges – it’s more of a theoretical curiosity that ensemble could create unnatural tokens. Standard BPE can also produce some strange tokens (especially byte-level BPE yields fragments of words or broken unicode pieces), yet models manage.

**6. Complexity of Tuning and Lack of Existing Tools:** Because this approach is not mainstream, there’s a lack of off-the-shelf support. It would likely require custom scripts to train multiple tokenizers and merge them, and careful tuning of parameters like how to weight each sub-tokenizer's contribution or deciding chunking strategy. One might need to try overlapping splits, various voting thresholds, etc., essentially introducing a new hyperparameter (the ensemble scheme) into the pipeline. Without literature to guide these choices, it could be a research project in itself to find an optimal method. Meanwhile, proven methods like Unigram LM tokenization or even heuristic vocabulary reallocations (like ensuring at least X tokens per language) might achieve many of the same benefits with less fuss.

In summary, **the case *against* an ensemble-trained BPE** is centered on practicality and uncertain benefit: it complicates the tokenizer training process significantly and might sacrifice the optimality of the vocabulary for the integrated corpus. It could exclude some genuinely useful tokens and thereby hurt compression or performance on certain inputs. The actual gains in model performance are not guaranteed to be large, given that models are robust to token variations[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.insights-1.7.pdf#:~:text=yield%20translation%20models%20that%20perform,it%20sug%02gests%20that%20variations%20on). Many of the issues ensemble aims to fix (like rare tokens, domain imbalance) can be addressed by simpler means: e.g., *Picky BPE* fixes rare tokens by a refinement step[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04599#:~:text=,in%20several%20cases%20improves%20it), and multilingual tokenizers can be improved by careful data sampling or by adding a small number of language-specific tokens post-hoc[gucci-j.github.io](https://gucci-j.github.io/post/en/vocab-expansion/#:~:text=Since%20the%20advent%20of%20LLaMA2%2C,resulting%20in%20better%20inference%20efficiency)[gucci-j.github.io](https://gucci-j.github.io/post/en/vocab-expansion/#:~:text=2,rules). Those approaches might achieve a similar end result (a balanced vocabulary) without needing multiple full training runs.

## Comparative Evaluation: Weighing the Evidence

To synthesize the above points, we provide a brief “scorecard” of how a standard single BPE tokenizer compares to an ensemble-of-BPEs approach on key criteria, based on the discussed evidence:

* **Compression on Training Data:** *Single BPE excels.* It greedily optimizes for compression on its training corpus, typically achieving very high compression rates on in-domain text[aiml.com](https://aiml.com/demystifying-byte-pair-encoding-bpe/#:~:text=print%28f%E2%80%9DTrain%20compression%3A%20%7Btrain_ratio%3A.2f%7D,%E2%80%9D). An ensemble BPE would likely compress the original corpus slightly less, since it might exclude some high-frequency merges that were local to one chunk. This is a point against ensemble if compression efficiency is the goal.
* **Generalization to New Domains:** *Ensemble potentially wins.* A single BPE often shows degraded tokenization efficiency on out-of-distribution text (longer sequences)[aiml.com](https://aiml.com/demystifying-byte-pair-encoding-bpe/#:~:text=2.%20Domain%20%26%20Distribution%20,resulting%20in%20longer%20token%20sequences). Ensemble tokenization is designed to be more agnostic, favoring common patterns across subsets. While we lack direct experimental proof, it stands to reason that an ensemble-selected vocabulary would yield **more stable tokenization across domains** – fewer out-of-vocab fragments – especially if the ensemble was constructed from varied data. In qualitative terms, we give ensemble a higher mark here, as it explicitly addresses domain diversity. (For example, an English BPE used on non-English text yields poor compression[aiml.com](https://aiml.com/demystifying-byte-pair-encoding-bpe/#:~:text=y,English%20Text%20Source%3A%20AIML.com%20Research), but an ensemble including non-English data would have tokens for those languages.)
* **Rare/Under-trained Token Reduction:** *Ensemble likely improves.* The ensemble approach inherently downplays tokens that aren’t frequent in multiple data portions, which correlates with being globally infrequent. As discussed, rare tokens are problematic[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04599#:~:text=,in%20several%20cases%20improves%20it), and methods that remove them have succeeded without loss[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04599#:~:text=reliable%20method,in%20several%20cases%20improves%20it). Therefore, an ensemble tokenizer would probably have fewer extremely low-frequency tokens than a single BPE of equal size (since those tend to be idiosyncratic). This is a plus for ensemble – fewer embedding vectors wasted on tokens the model sees only a few times[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04599#:~:text=reliable%20method,in%20several%20cases%20improves%20it). In essence, ensemble acts as a kind of regularization on the vocabulary frequency distribution.
* **Linguistic Alignment and Morpheme Quality:** *Slight edge to ensemble?* Single BPE’s greedy merges can produce some splits that ignore morphology (like merging across morpheme boundaries because of corpus frequency). An ensemble might filter out some odd merges if they’re not consistently found. One can speculate that an ensemble vocabulary could align more with true subwords, similar to how Unigram LM (which effectively considers global likelihood) did better in linguistic segmentation[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.414#:~:text=pretraining,over%20the%20more%20prevalent%20BPE). However, without direct evidence, this advantage is tentative. It depends how the ensemble is done. If weighted equally on diverse text, it might indeed prioritize more generally useful morphemes (like prefixes/suffixes that appear in many contexts). So we cautiously say ensemble could be better in capturing “universal” subword units, but this is not guaranteed.
* **Multilingual Fairness and Efficacy:** *Ensemble offers control.* A single joint BPE tends to favor larger languages, resulting in vocabulary overlap that can hurt language-specific tasks[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=vocabulary%20overlap%20observed%20in%20sub,in%20the%20multilin%02gual%20vocabulary%20significantly). An ensemble (one model per language or per family) with equal voting would ensure each language contributes a quota of tokens, improving *vocabulary allocation* for smaller languages. This would likely improve tasks that need language-specific tokens (POS, etc.)[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=Notably%2C%20the%20model%20instance%20with,for%20tokens%20that%20is%20especially), as evidenced by the success of NoOverlap in those tasks. On cross-lingual tasks, however, some overlap is beneficial[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=vocabulary%20overlap%20observed%20in%20sub,in%20the%20multilin%02gual%20vocabulary%20significantly); an ensemble can also accommodate that by naturally sharing tokens that multiple languages independently created (e.g., shared scripts, digits, proper nouns). So ensemble provides a *tunable overlap*. Overall, for a multilingual model aiming to support many languages fairly, an ensemble approach is advantageous in principle. It’s essentially a way to implement a more multilingual-aware tokenization without manually crafting the vocabulary per language. We score ensemble higher for multilingual settings. (Note: if one only cares about overall multilingual MLM perplexity, a single BPE might be slightly better because it’s optimized for aggregate, but the ensemble’s equitable approach is often preferred for fairness and per-language performance[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=Notably%2C%20the%20model%20instance%20with,for%20tokens%20that%20is%20especially)[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=NOOVERLAP%20approach%20obtains%20competitive%20results,We%20think).)
* **Downstream Model Performance:** *Likely comparable, minor differences.* Given the literature, we expect that for large models, both approaches would yield similar performance on average, with differences manifesting in specific areas (as noted: slight boost for specific tasks with ensemble, or slight drop in perplexity for ensemble due to less compression). For example, Bostrom & Durrett saw a consistent but small advantage for Unigram (a more global approach) over BPE in downstream tasks[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.414#:~:text=pretraining,over%20the%20more%20prevalent%20BPE)[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.414#:~:text=with%20morphology%20and%20avoids%20problems,we%20find%20that%20the). By analogy, an ensemble (more global than greedy BPE) might similarly offer small gains in certain tasks. The random-BPE results show that not all variations matter[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.insights-1.7.pdf#:~:text=yield%20translation%20models%20that%20perform,it%20sug%02gests%20that%20variations%20on). So we’re not expecting a dramatic change. If one had to assign a score, one might say: both single and ensemble can achieve strong results, with ensemble having a slight edge in scenarios where tokenization of specific rare strings was a limiting factor, and single BPE having an edge in raw modeling of very frequent idiosyncrasies (though models often handle those anyway). In short, **neither approach clearly dominates in overall language modeling performance without considering specific use-cases**. This is essentially a wash or a mild point for ensemble if your use-case aligns with its strengths (like cross-domain generality).
* **Complexity and Computational Cost:** *Single BPE is far simpler.* This is a big strike against ensemble: you need to train multiple tokenizers (which is already time-consuming on large data), store multiple merge lists, and implement a merging algorithm with potential manual tuning. Single BPE is one-and-done and has many existing implementations. Ensemble would multiply the training cost by M (if done naively) or at least add overhead. Unless you have parallel resources and a very clear need, this extra complexity is hard to justify. So on a practicality score, the single approach wins easily.

To visualize a qualitative “scorecard”: if we assign a plus (+), neutral (0), or minus (–) for ensemble vs single on each dimension (assuming single as baseline):

* **Compression (in-domain)**: Ensemble (–) vs Single (+) – ensemble likely yields slightly worse compression of training data due to not using all specific merges[aiml.com](https://aiml.com/demystifying-byte-pair-encoding-bpe/#:~:text=print%28f%E2%80%9DTrain%20compression%3A%20%7Btrain_ratio%3A.2f%7D,%E2%80%9D)[aiml.com](https://aiml.com/demystifying-byte-pair-encoding-bpe/#:~:text=,resulting%20in%20slightly%20worse%20compression).
* **Generalization (cross-domain)**: Ensemble (+) vs Single (–/0) – ensemble is designed to generalize better, whereas single can falter out-of-domain[aiml.com](https://aiml.com/demystifying-byte-pair-encoding-bpe/#:~:text=y,English%20Text%20Source%3A%20AIML.com%20Research).
* **Rare token handling**: Ensemble (+) vs Single (–) – ensemble prunes rare tokens by consensus, single includes them (leading to under-trained tokens)[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04599#:~:text=reliable%20method,in%20several%20cases%20improves%20it).
* **Multilingual/domain coverage**: Ensemble (+) vs Single (–) – ensemble can ensure each domain language gets tokens, single skews to dominant data[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=In%20this%20paper%2C%20we%20propose,We%20also%20observe%20that%20the)[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=Notably%2C%20the%20model%20instance%20with,for%20tokens%20that%20is%20especially).
* **Model performance**: Ensemble (0/+) vs Single (0) – likely comparable overall, with slight edge to ensemble in certain tasks (morphology-rich, balanced multilingual)[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=Notably%2C%20the%20model%20instance%20with,for%20tokens%20that%20is%20especially).
* **Implementation complexity**: Ensemble (–) vs Single (+) – ensemble is far more complex.

This rough rubric suggests ensemble tokenization can *outperform the single approach on qualitative criteria like generality and balance*, but at the cost of **higher complexity and potentially a small hit to optimal compression**. The magnitude of these pros and cons is important: the improvements might be modest, and the complexity is significant.

## Conclusion and Outlook

Considering all the above evidence, **can a BPE tokenizer be improved via an ensemble of tokenizers?** The idea has merit in theory, addressing genuine shortcomings of the standard BPE. It aligns with successful concepts like vocabulary smoothing (removing rare tokens)[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04599#:~:text=reliable%20method,in%20several%20cases%20improves%20it) and balanced vocabulary allocation in multilingual settings[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=Notably%2C%20the%20model%20instance%20with,for%20tokens%20that%20is%20especially). An ensemble approach could naturally achieve these by pooling knowledge from multiple data segments. Especially for use-cases where **generalization to varied text or fairness across languages** is paramount, an ensemble-derived vocabulary might be beneficial. For example, a multilingual tokenizer built by ensembling per-language BPEs could ensure no language is left without common words in the vocab, solving issues of smaller languages being ignored[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=In%20this%20paper%2C%20we%20propose,We%20also%20observe%20that%20the)[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=Notably%2C%20the%20model%20instance%20with,for%20tokens%20that%20is%20especially). Likewise, a tokenizer for a general AI assistant that sees many domains might do well to ensemble domain-specific tokenizers so that medical terms, legal jargon, and casual speech all have some representation in its subwords.

However, the drawbacks are non-trivial. There is currently no standard algorithm to perform this ensemble merging optimally, and one risks either complicating the workflow or ending up close to what a single unified BPE would have done anyway. In fact, a single BPE *can* approximate some ensemble effects by simply stratifying input data (e.g., oversampling low-resource domains so that their patterns reach higher frequency). This might be a simpler way to achieve a balanced vocabulary: explicitly weight your corpus to reflect the importance of each portion, then run standard BPE. This essentially gives some of the benefits of an ensemble (because BPE will pick up tokens from each portion proportionally to its weighted frequency) without needing to merge models post-hoc. For instance, one could concatenate 10% of each domain data, even if the actual corpus fraction is smaller, to force BPE to include those domain terms. This approach is frequently used in multilingual tokenizer training (upsampling minority languages). It’s not as algorithmically neat as ensembling multiple independent models, but it is effective and simpler.

The empirical evidence we have suggests that **the performance differences might be small in practice**. The subword segmentation chosen is not the dominant factor in large-scale model success as long as it's reasonable (randomized merges didn’t significantly derail models[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.insights-1.7.pdf#:~:text=yield%20translation%20models%20that%20perform,it%20sug%02gests%20that%20variations%20on)). That said, certain edge cases do matter: extremely poor tokenization of a particular script or an abundance of rare tokens can hurt a model (e.g., tokens that never appear in pretraining will be randomly initialized and can become *unknown traps*[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04599#:~:text=,in%20several%20cases%20improves%20it)). These are precisely the problems an ensemble or refined tokenizer would fix. So, an ensemble tokenizer might not make a big difference on average, but could avoid worst-case issues and improve worst-case performance.

From a *belief* or subjective standpoint: I would conclude that **the ensemble approach is an intriguing but complex solution**. I believe it could be valuable when one has sharply distinct data domains or languages that one wants to represent fairly in the vocabulary – essentially as a way to enforce a more equitable or generalized token set. The literature on multilingual tokenization provides evidence that a more balanced approach (like NoOverlap or partial sharing) yields better results in some tasks[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=Notably%2C%20the%20model%20instance%20with,for%20tokens%20that%20is%20especially)[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=NOOVERLAP%20approach%20obtains%20competitive%20results,We%20think), which supports the core rationale for ensembling. On the other hand, if one’s goal is purely to maximize model perplexity on a known distribution, a single BPE (or a globally optimized tokenizer like Unigram LM) will be hard to beat. The ensemble might slightly lag there because it forgoes some compression of the majority in favor of the minority.

In terms of *scoring the idea*: If we rate its viability, I’d give it a moderate score. It’s **theoretically sound** in addressing known issues (+1), and **supported by analogous research** (like Picky BPE, multilingual studies) that show benefits (+1). But it’s **not proven directly** (0), and **practically cumbersome** (-1). The net outcome might be neutral to mildly positive for certain applications. It’s probably *not* a game-changer for general NLP, which is why we haven’t seen papers explicitly on “ensemble BPE”. Instead, researchers have tackled these problems in other ways (global optimization of vocab, vocabulary expansion techniques, etc.).

One could view ensemble BPE training as a form of **ensemble learning at the preprocessing level** – a creative concept that might yield incremental improvements. If one were conducting a literature review or initial hypothesis test (as the user alludes to), the recommendation would be: *Try it on a controlled setup.* For example, take a corpus, split it, ensemble train a tokenizer, and compare it to a regular tokenizer on metrics like compression, vocabulary overlap with each subset, and downstream model performance. That experiment would concretely reveal the trade-offs. Given the evidence we surveyed, I would expect the ensemble tokenizer to show: slightly lower compression on the original data, more even coverage of each subset (each subset’s text has maybe shorter tokenization length than with the single BPE, except the majority subset which might get a bit worse), and roughly equal downstream performance with possibly better results on subset-specific evaluations.

In conclusion, **the idea of an ensemble-trained BPE tokenizer is plausible and addresses real issues, but it is not a widely adopted practice**. It holds appeal for specialized scenarios (multilingual, multi-domain, low-resource generalization) and the literature indirectly supports its potential benefits[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.350.pdf#:~:text=Notably%2C%20the%20model%20instance%20with,for%20tokens%20that%20is%20especially)[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04599#:~:text=reliable%20method,in%20several%20cases%20improves%20it). On the other hand, simpler or more principled methods (like the unigram model or careful data weighting) may achieve similar ends with less complexity. The evidence weighed above leans slightly in favor of the concept *in principle* – it is likely to produce a “better” tokenizer by certain measures (generalization, balance). Yet, the improvement may be modest and must be balanced against the effort required to implement and validate such a tokenizer.

Ultimately, whether one should pursue an ensemble BPE approach depends on the use-case priorities: if you absolutely need a tokenizer that works well across very diverse text and you’re willing to invest in the complexity, it could be worthwhile. If maximum efficiency on a known domain is the goal, sticking to a single BPE (perhaps with minor tweaks) is probably the better course. In the spirit of casting a wide net and initial hypothesis, this idea certainly merits further exploration, but any gains must be measured against the backdrop of existing evidence that many tokenization variations yield comparable outcomes[aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2023.insights-1.7.pdf#:~:text=yield%20translation%20models%20that%20perform,it%20sug%02gests%20that%20variations%20on). The concept is a valuable piece in the ongoing discussion that tokenization is *not* a solved problem and can significantly influence model capabilities[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04653v1#:~:text=primary%20way%20text%20gets%20tokenized,aspect%20of%20AI%20research%20in). Ensembling might be one of several routes to make tokenizers more robust and adaptive in the future.
